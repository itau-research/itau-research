# A Proposal for Livelihood Security Reform Ahead of AGI-Driven Employment Contraction

**Author:** Genki Takatori (writing as "itau"), independent researcher, Japan
**Original paper:** November 6, 2026 — 来たるべきAGIによる雇用縮小に向けた生活保障の制度改革の提案 (https://note.com/arsenal_best/n/n2d55b0237b7d) (Japanese)
**This summary:** November 6, 2026. One page, written to be found, read, and cited.

---

## The problem

The paper's premise is a society in which AI takes over production and private employment keeps shrinking, without new industries restoring aggregate employment. It deliberately does not make a recovery of private employment a condition of the design.

The danger it targets is not a shortage of productive capacity. Capacity can remain while distribution fails: if wages — the main channel through which people receive the output of production — contract, demand falls, arrears spread, firms go bankrupt, and maintenance stops, until actual supply is destroyed by the interruption of distribution. The current system works in the wrong direction under this change: the more support is needed, the more labor-tax revenue shrinks. The success criterion is concrete — that people can keep using food, housing, medical care, and electricity. Because institutional preparation takes years, the paper argues for starting before nationwide mass unemployment is confirmed in the statistics.

## The scheme, designed as one system

The proposal legislates livelihood security, useful public employment, a universal basic dividend, public attribution of capital returns and equity, and investment in livelihood supply as a single package — production, distribution, livelihood supply, and transition-period financing treated as one system, not separate programs.

- **Basic dividend.** $1,200 per year to each of 250 million adults ($300 billion in total), paid regardless of employment. It is a floor of universal participation in the output of production, explicitly not a living income on its own.
- **Additional security indexed to livelihood costs.** For those who lose income, a top-up calculated from a livelihood-cost basket — food, housing, medical care, electricity and heat, water, mobility, education and communications, set by household composition and region — minus the person's income, available public services, and the dividend. In the benchmark case, a non-participant's cash is $36,000 including the dividend (a top-up of $34,800). Eligibility turns on current income and household circumstances, not on proving AI caused the job loss, with provisional payment targeted within 14 days.
- **Public employment as one component.** Jobs are built up from identified needs and delivery capacity, not from a headcount target. The benchmark model sets participation at 30% — in the paper's 30-million-person case, 9 million people. (The first version assumed 60%; the revision cut it to 30% and states plainly that the figure is an illustration, not a capacity guarantee.) Public-employment wages are kept separate from the dividend and the guarantee, so livelihood payments continue even where job intake lags.
- **No cliff between work and benefits.** The top-up tapers as earnings rise — additional benefit = max(0, 34,800 − 0.55 × wage) — with a design target of keeping combined effective burden rates at or below 70%, so that taking work always raises cash income.
- **A national fund built from equity contributions.** Companies above a certain size contribute shares annually to an independent national fund — the worked example uses 2.5% of post-issuance shares per year — giving the public a continuing claim on production outcomes independent of employment. Issuing new shares means the state need not buy assets with cash; the burden falls mainly as dilution of existing holdings. Governance is to be independent of politics, with diversified holdings and disclosed conflicts of interest.

## The model case, as a stress test

The paper is explicit that its numbers are durability tests of the institution, not employment forecasts or probabilities. It models 10, 30, and 60 million people (annual-average equivalents) losing private income, assuming an original salary of $80,000. In the central 30-million case, original wages of $2.4 trillion become avoidable labor costs of $2.8 trillion; AI substitution costs $0.7 trillion, leaving a $2.1 trillion cost difference at substituting firms, which the scheme tracks into profits, price reductions, and public recovery (a symmetric 50% recovery yields $1.05 trillion). Revised-model spending is $2.11 trillion, and the funding requirement for the newly affected population is $2.5405 trillion — before the further cost of extending the same guarantee to people already outside the newly displaced group. Even so, average cash reaches only 68.25% of the original take-home pay. The fund builds slowly: in the all-equity example, with covered company value fixed at $50 trillion, year-10 cash is about $330 billion — early livelihood security must rest on tax revenue and realized cash, not on the fund.

## Key assumptions and failure conditions

- **Everything is a design assumption.** Headcounts, benefit levels, the 30% participation rate, unit costs, the 50% recovery rate, and the fund's payout ratios are fixed-price comparison parameters, not measured estimates. Family medical, housing, and child costs are not yet fully stacked into the totals; the paper calls for recalculation from tax and household data before any formal budget.
- **The fund's returns must not be pre-spent.** Its projected cash assumes constant covered company value and historical payout ratios, flagged in the paper as sensitivity examples. The guarantee must not depend on the fund growing as projected.
- **Distribution must track uneven households.** Price declines do not fall evenly, so any consumption-tax recapture is paired with refunds and benefits based on the livelihood basket — and the same decline is never counted twice, once as revenue and once as lower benefit need.
- **Speed is a binding constraint.** Absorbing 30 million people over 36 months means adding support pathways for roughly 830,000 people per month. The design is tested against intake speed, not only totals.
